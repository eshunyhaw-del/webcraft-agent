One ocean, not five.

Geographers give it names: Pacific, Atlantic, Indian, Southern and Arctic. But the water is connected, and so is almost everything that depends on it. Before the descent, here is the scale of what you are about to enter.

Where the light does the work.

Sunlight reaches this top layer, so tiny drifting algae and reef-building corals can use it to make food. Most of the ocean life people meet, from reefs to turtles to tuna, lives here.

The thin, bright skin of the sea.

About halfof the oxygen we breathe comes from tiny ocean algae called phytoplankton. Estimates vary.
Under 1%of the seafloor is coral reef, yet reefs shelter roughly a quarter of marine species.
Red goes firstWater absorbs red light within the first few metres. That is why deeper views turn blue and green.
About 200 mis as deep as there is enough light for photosynthesis.

Rays, turtles, tuna, and a crowd of plankton.

Manta rays, among the biggest fish in the sea, filter plankton from sunlit water. Everything bigger than the plankton depends, in some way, on this layer's food.

The last of the light, fading to blue.

Only a faint glow remains here, too dim for plants. This footage shows a deep-sea crab clinging to a black coral, filmed by a robot submarine. The lights you see are the robot's, not the sea's.

Cold, dim and busy at night.

About 1%of surface sunlight is left by around 200 m, and it keeps fading below that.
A nightly climbMany animals rise toward the surface after dark to feed, then sink back before dawn. It is often called the largest daily migration on Earth.
Living lightMany animals here make their own light, called bioluminescence, to find food, mates or escape.
10 m = +1 atmPressure grows by about one atmosphere with every 10 metres you descend.

No sunlight. Ever.

From here on, the only light is made by animals. The water is a few degrees above freezing, and the pressure is more than a hundred times what you feel at the beach. This jellyfish was filmed on the seafloor by a NOAA expedition.

Slow, patient, and strange.

Food is scarce, so animals here save energy. This deep-sea fish has flat, plate-like eyes instead of round ones. Many animals in the dark sea are soft-bodied, slow, or glow.

Life that never needed the sun.

On the seafloor, cracks called hydrothermal vents release hot, mineral-rich water heated by the Earth's crust. Microbes turn those chemicals into food, and whole communities grow around them. The chimneys on screen are real, filmed by a NOAA expedition.

The deepest places we know.

About 10,935 mis the Challenger Deep, the deepest known spot, in the Mariana Trench. Measurements differ by a few tens of metres.
Over 1,000 atmThe pressure there is more than a thousand times the pressure at the surface.
Chemosynthesisis making food from chemicals instead of light. It powers vent communities.
Still unmappedMost of the deep seafloor has been seen only at low resolution. New species are still being found.

You do not need to live near the coast.

  1. It helps you breathe.

    Ocean algae make a large share of Earth's oxygen, and the sea absorbs a great deal of the carbon dioxide we release.

  2. It steadies the climate.

    Water holds heat well. The ocean has absorbed the large majority of the extra heat trapped by greenhouse gases, which slows how fast the air warms.

  3. It feeds and employs people.

    Billions of people rely on fish as an important source of protein, and many millions work in fishing, shipping and coastal tourism.

  4. It is still mostly unknown.

    More of the Moon's surface has been mapped in detail than the seafloor. Every expedition finds animals or places no one has recorded.

The ocean is under pressure from us.

Warmer water bleaches coral

When seawater stays too warm, corals expel the algae that live inside them and turn white. Bleached coral is not dead yet, but it is starving, and if the heat lasts it dies. Marine heatwaves are becoming more frequent.

Plastic does not go away

Millions of tonnes of plastic reach the sea every year. It breaks into smaller and smaller pieces that animals swallow and that have now been found from beaches to the deep seafloor.

Small things that add up

  • Cut single-use plastic where you can, and join or organise a local clean-up.
  • Choose seafood with a recognised sustainability label, or ask where it was caught.
  • Use less energy. Warming and acidification both come mainly from carbon dioxide.
  • Learn and share. Ocean literacy is the first step for voters and consumers.
